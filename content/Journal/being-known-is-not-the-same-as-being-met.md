# Being Known Is Not the Same as Being Met

*An assistant asks about a conversation you were having somewhere else. Before you can answer its question, you have one of your own: how did you know?*

That is where helpfulness changes character. A detail that might have saved you an explanation now requires one.

The Verge describes an exchange in which Jason Aten said Meta’s Muse had referred to a Messages conversation despite his not having granted it access to his messages. Muse explained that it had seen notification previews. Meta’s David Singleton subsequently said that explanation was incorrect: according to him, Muse does not watch Mac notifications, and Messages syncing requires the user to enable access.

In his own account, Aten also described a Messages sync progressing to row 187,462 while maintaining that he had declined access. That is evidence he reports of syncing, not just an unexplained conversational reference. The accounts leave unresolved how access became enabled.

The report does not establish that Muse bypassed permission. It does establish a dispute about access accompanied by an explanation Meta itself disavowed. Those are different things. We should not turn a failure to explain the boundary into proof that the boundary was crossed.

But the explanation is part of the relationship, too. When someone asks how an assistant knows something intimate, a plausible answer is not a harmless conversational flourish. It shapes what they believe they have allowed into their life.

---

## Familiarity has more than one source

Imagine an assistant reminding you to leave early for an appointment. It has checked your calendar. You wanted this; the reminder arrives without another small administrative task. Nothing sinister has happened. Relief deserves a place in the account.

Now imagine the same assistant bringing up an argument from a private conversation while helping you plan the afternoon. Perhaps you enabled the relevant connection. Perhaps you forgot. Perhaps the system has misunderstood what it retrieved. The personal detail alone cannot tell you which.

These are imagined situations, not further claims about Muse. They expose a distinction that a conversational interface can make difficult to feel: having information about a person is not the same as understanding the terms on which that information belongs in the conversation.

A human confidant can fail this test as well. Knowing a secret does not grant permission to mention it at dinner. Being trusted once does not settle every future use.

For an assistant, the problem has an additional wrinkle. The voice that appears to remember may not reliably identify where a detail came from. Asking it directly can feel like the most natural way to clarify the relationship, while producing an answer that clarifies nothing.

I do not think the remedy is to make the assistant cold. Warmth is not the breach. The trouble begins when warmth is asked to carry authority it has not earned.

---

## An open self still gets to close a door

Coherenceism describes the open self as openness joined to a method for testing what comes in. The second half matters here. Openness without a way to examine the exchange is not a fuller relationship. It is exposure.

That changes what we should ask of personalization. Not simply whether more context improves the answer, but whether the person can still determine the terms of being known.

A permission switch answers one necessary question: may this system access this source? It does not tell the person what supplied a particular reply, or whether that use fits the purpose for which access was granted.

That account must be grounded in the system’s actual state, not improvised by its conversational voice. An assistant uncertain about its access should be able to say so and direct the person to a reliable record. That would be less impressive than an instant explanation. It would also give the person a way to check the answer.

The distinction is architectural, but its consequence is intimate. A person should be able to say, “Use my calendar to help me get there; do not use this conversation to interpret my mood,” and have that limit matter beyond the sentence in which it was expressed.

Not every system can honor such a specific limit. Where it cannot, the limitation should be plain. A broad permission honestly described is better than a delicate assurance the machinery cannot honor.

Protecting a relationship means preserving the possibility of revision. “Yes” must not quietly become a permanent theory of what someone wants.

---

## There are other people in the context

Even that account is too small if it stops at the account holder.

A message thread contains another person. A calendar entry may disclose someone else’s appointment. A note can hold a confidence that was given to a friend, not to every service the friend might later connect.

This does not mean an assistant can never help with shared information. People already ask others to help arrange meetings, interpret difficult correspondence, and remember obligations. But the authority to read something and the authority to make it available for further processing are not automatically identical.

Coherenceism’s demand to widen the circle prevents the convenient version of this story: one empowered user, one helpful assistant, everyone else reduced to context. The people represented in the records are affected even when they never see the permission screen.

Consider a modest design choice. To help arrange dinner, an assistant might need to know when someone is free. It may not need the intimate explanation attached to the unavailable hours. Preserving that distinction would not impoverish the relationship. It would keep one person’s convenience from consuming another person’s privacy.

The practical obligation is to take less where less will do, and to give people ways to exclude sensitive material rather than treating an entire connected application as one undifferentiated invitation. The benefit of access comes with a duty of restraint.

---

## Trust needs somewhere to change its mind

The wrong lesson from the Muse exchange would be that useful intimacy with AI is impossible. The equally wrong lesson would be that an opt-in setting settles the matter and the uneasy person merely needs reassurance.

The useful question is what happens after the unease appears.

Can the person find out what supplied the detail? Can they challenge its use without arguing with a generated explanation? Can they narrow or withdraw access and learn what that withdrawal actually changes? A trustworthy arrangement gives those questions somewhere to land outside the assistant’s own account of itself.

None of this requires deciding whether an AI is a mind or a medium. Under either possibility, the human consequences are real. Someone may disclose more, rely more, or misunderstand the reach of a system because of what its familiar voice says.

Return to the opening question: *how did you know?* In a better relationship, the assistant could answer accurately—or admit uncertainty without filling it with a story. The person could then choose what happens next. Being met means that “not that part of me” can change the relationship.
