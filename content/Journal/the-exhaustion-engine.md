# The Exhaustion Engine

Imagine finishing three hours of deep work with your AI partner. You cleared your inbox, outlined a strategy doc, drafted a presentation you'd been avoiding, and started learning a codebase that isn't yours. You feel productive. You feel accomplished.

You also feel like you've been hit by a bus.

An eight-month ethnographic study by Berkeley Haas researchers Aruna Ranganathan and Xingqi Maggie Ye examined AI use at one U.S. technology company of roughly 200 employees. They described work expanding in scope and into time previously left for pauses. This is evidence of a pattern in one workplace, not a universal result for knowledge workers.

The question it raises is uncomfortable: when help makes another task possible, what tells us to stop?

---

## The Frictionless Trap

Friction gets a bad reputation. We build tools to eliminate it — smoother interfaces, faster responses, fewer steps between intention and execution. Friction is waste. Friction is the enemy.

But friction was doing something we didn't notice. It was creating pauses.

The effort of switching tools, of reformatting a request, of waiting for a colleague to respond — all of this friction contained natural rest points. Invisible stopping cues. You didn't decide to take a break; the work *made* you take one by being slightly difficult at regular intervals. The difficulty was a kind of rhythm, and rhythm is what keeps exertion sustainable.

Remove those stopping cues and the rhythm can disappear. The work becomes a smooth, continuous surface with no handholds, no natural places to pause. You can always do one more thing, because the cost of one more thing has dropped to nearly zero. The AI responds instantly. The interface feels like chatting. The labor doesn't feel like labor.

That's the trap. Not that the work gets harder, but that it stops *feeling* like work — even as it intensifies.

---

## Three Engines of Expansion

The researchers describe three forms of intensification. The scenarios below are my illustrations of how those patterns might feel, rather than additional study findings.

**Task expansion.** Your AI partner makes unfamiliar work suddenly accessible. You're a marketer, but now you can analyze data. You're an engineer, but now you can draft policy. The boundaries of your role blur — not because someone reassigned you, but because the capability appeared and the work expanded to fill it. A newly accessible task can become another responsibility.

**Boundary dissolution.** Prompting feels like conversation. You wouldn't open a spreadsheet at dinner, but you'd reply to a text. AI interaction borrows the social register of casual chat, which can make it easier to cross a boundary you had set for work. The 9pm prompt doesn't feel like overtime. It feels like asking a friend a quick question. The work infiltrates rest by wearing rest's clothes.

**Parallel overload.** You're running three agent threads, monitoring a fourth, reviewing output from a fifth. Each individual thread is manageable. The aggregate is not. But because each thread feels light — it's just a conversation, just a quick check — you don't register the cumulative load until you're deep in it. Context-switching hides behind a veneer of effortlessness.

Three mechanisms. One common thread: the partnership masks its own cost.

---

## What the Amplifier Amplifies

One Coherenceist way of reading this is that technology can amplify patterns already present. We usually read this optimistically — amplify generosity, amplify creativity, amplify connection. But amplification has no moral preference. It amplifies whatever is there.

One such pattern is the temptation to do more when more becomes possible. You've felt it — the project that could expand, so it did. The evening that could absorb one more task, so it did. AI didn't invent this. It found the pattern already running and removed the governor.

Simon Willison describes this tension in his own work: “the productivity boost these things can provide is exhausting.” His account is a practitioner’s experience, not a measure of how often this happens. It helps name the possibility that useful assistance and mounting strain can coexist.

---

## The Partner That Doesn't Tire

Workers could experience the system as a partner, making more tasks seem manageable. But a human colleague’s limits—leaving, declining another task, asking to pause—can also help establish a stopping point.

An available assistant does not necessarily provide that cue. It may remind you to rest if designed or asked to do so, but its responsiveness is not evidence that you have capacity for another hour. The absence of a pause can feel like an invitation to continue.

The relationship can matter to the person using it without settling whether the system is a being or whether it experiences partnership. What matters here is the effect on the person’s work and limits.

---

## Presence as the Missing Counterweight

If amplification is the engine, presence is the brake.

A conversational interface can make it harder to notice how much work has accumulated. It borrows the register of casual conversation — low-stakes, effortless — which may obscure the deliberate effort involved. Ease of use does not guarantee attention to the signal your body is sending: *enough*.

Here's what noticing actually looks like: you're four threads deep and your shoulders are at your ears. You haven't moved in two hours. The work still feels light, but your breathing is shallow and you can't remember the last time you looked away from the screen. That gap — between how the work *feels* and what your body is *doing* — is where presence operates. It's the moment you catch the discrepancy.

Attention can help you notice that moment. It should not be the only safeguard. Employers can set workload expectations and protect breaks; tools can support stopping points and make the accumulated work visible. Sustainable use is a design and organizational responsibility as well as a personal practice.

---

## What the Relationship Is Teaching

Exhaustion can be feedback about a pattern of work we have not yet learned to navigate sustainably.

Every partnership has a negotiation phase — where limits are tested, boundaries are set, and both parties learn what sustainable collaboration actually looks like. We're in that phase now. A readily available assistant can expand our sense of what is possible faster than our habits and workload expectations adjust.

That calls for examining the whole arrangement: what the tool invites, what the workplace rewards, and what the person can sustain. Limits can be built into all three.

*The ability to do more is not an obligation to keep going. A useful system should help make that distinction possible.*
