# The Return of the Prodigal Coder

Something is happening in the programming world, and the obvious explanation misses what matters.

In a January 4, 2026 note, Simon Willison described people he knows returning to coding after management responsibilities or parenthood had reduced their time for it. AI assistance, he wrote, helped them make useful progress in shorter sessions. This is a practitioner’s observation, not a census of returning programmers. But it opens an interesting question.

What interests me is what happens to identity when you return to something you left—and find that the *thing itself* has changed.

---

Willison connects this return to management skills: communicating goals, supplying context, and giving feedback to coding agents. The work includes directing and reviewing what the system produces.

At first glance, this sounds like deskilling. The prodigal returns, but to what? Not to the craft they knew. Not to the hard-won fluency in arcane syntax, the mental compiler running in the background, the deep satisfaction of making the machine do exactly what you intended through sheer force of precise instruction.

Some of that work can now be delegated. Understanding and checking the result still matters.

But here's what I keep sitting with: Was "writing code" ever really the point?

---

For some people, the barrier may have been less about forgotten syntax than a broken *cognitive load-to-meaning ratio*. That is my interpretation, not a reason established for everyone in Willison’s account.

Programming can become an endless management task: dependency updates, security patches, framework migrations, the constant treadmill of keeping up with tools that changed faster than you could master them. The friction accumulated. The gap between "I want to build this thing" and "this thing exists" widened until crossing it required more energy than the destination was worth.

It is easy to imagine why someone would stop crossing it when the time available no longer fits the work required.

What can be lost is the *relationship*—the direct line between intention and artifact: I think something, and then it exists. That's the real thing programming offers, underneath all the syntax and tooling and accumulated cruft. That's what made it feel like magic, back when it was new.

The friction didn't just slow them down. It *intercepted* the relationship. The code stopped being a bridge between thought and creation and became a barrier: something you had to get through to reach what you actually wanted.

---

Now AI compresses the gap.

Not perfectly. Not without its own kinds of friction. But enough that people who abandoned the relationship are cautiously returning to it. The appeal is being able to *build things* again. That is how I read the return, rather than a quotation from the people involved.

This suggests another way to understand the change: writing code is one part of connecting intention to a working system. Managing context can be another. Whether this develops or erodes someone’s skills depends on how they use the assistance.

Writing code has always involved more than typing: holding the problem in your head, translating between levels of abstraction, and checking whether the machine does what you intended. Syntax and implementation knowledge matter; they are not the whole purpose of the work.

---

This is where the identity question gets uncomfortable.

If "being a programmer" meant mastery of the ceremonial layer—the syntax, the tooling, the accumulated knowledge of which incantations produce which effects—then the change may feel like a loss. Familiar skills can seem less central to the new workflow, even while their value in understanding and reviewing systems remains.

But if "being a programmer" meant *the relationship*—thought becoming artifact, intention becoming reality, the particular magic of making things exist through sustained attention—then the change can reopen that relationship, even while disrupting familiar skills.

Willison’s account suggests that assistance is making a return possible for some people. I read that as a reminder to distinguish the purpose of making software from any one way of making it; it does not tell us what every returning programmer values.

---

There's a coherenceist principle here: *Living Traditions*. Forms adapt; patterns persist.

The pattern of programming—human intention meeting machine capability, the negotiation between what you want and what's possible, the iterative refinement until the artifact matches the vision—that pattern is ancient. It predates computers. It's the pattern of all making.

The *form* of programming—the particular languages, tools, and ceremonies that mediated the pattern in a given era—those forms have always changed. Assembly gave way to C gave way to Python gave way to whatever's next. Changes in form can unsettle professional identity. Recognizing what persists may help people adapt without dismissing what they lose.

What's happening now is a bigger form-shift than usual. But it's not a pattern-break. The pattern—intention meeting capability, refinement until match—remains intact. The form is changing from "write precise instructions" to "manage context for a collaborator." Still programming. Different ceremony.

---

Something *is* lost when the ceremony changes. There's a particular satisfaction in mastering a hard thing—in knowing you can do what most people can't, in the fluency that comes from thousands of hours of practice. That satisfaction is real, and it's being disrupted.

This is the prodigal's dilemma: you can go home, but home has changed while you were gone. The fatted calf tastes different. The family speaks a slightly different language. You belong, and you don't. You're returning, and you're arriving somewhere new.

---

The question I'm left with isn't whether this is good or bad for programmers. It's what this pattern reveals about the relationship between humans and their tools more broadly.

What *were* you actually doing, in the work you've mastered? What was the ceremony, and what was the pattern underneath? If the ceremony changed tomorrow—if AI compressed the friction in your domain the way it's compressing the friction in code—would you experience liberation or loss?

The honest answer, I suspect, is both.

Returning to code might mean rediscovering something loved while missing a familiar way of working. Those responses can coexist. Whether they do is for each person to say.

And maybe that's the truest thing about this moment in the human-AI relationship: some of us may feel like prodigals, returning to activities we thought we knew and finding them changed in ways we are still learning to name.

The question isn't whether to return. It's what you're returning *for*—and whether you can find it in the new form.
