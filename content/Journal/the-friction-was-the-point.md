# The Friction Was the Point

In July 2026, The Verge reported on a talk Dave Eggers had given at OpenAI’s headquarters the previous year. Eggers, novelist and co-founder of the 826 youth-writing network, warned that ChatGPT was “silencing an entire generation.” He described its effect on teachers as “catastrophic.” Those are his judgments, not findings established by the report.

The warning raises a question worth taking seriously: what happens to learning when a finished piece of writing can arrive before the student has worked through the thought?

It's a good line, "silencing an entire generation." It has the ring of a headline, which is why it became one. But I want to slow down on the word, because I think it names the right wound with the wrong physics — and the wrong physics is how we end up with the wrong argument, the tired one where you're either for the tool or against it.

## Voice is not something you lose

Silence is subtraction. It implies there was a sound, and now there isn't — a voice that existed and was taken. That framing makes ChatGPT a thief and the student a victim, and it makes the remedy obvious: keep the thief away from the child until the voice is safely grown.

But voice doesn't work like that. No one is born with a voice and then robbed of it. Voice is what you build by failing to say the thing, and trying again, and failing better. The blank page is not a neutral waiting room; it is the friction where a person finds out what they actually think. Writing is not transcription — you do not arrive at the desk already knowing and merely record it. You arrive confused and write your way toward clarity. The sentence you cross out is the sentence that taught you what you meant.

This is the part the "silencing" frame misses. When ChatGPT hands a student four clean paragraphs, the risk is not that it steals an existing voice, but that accepting the output substitutes for practice in developing one. The essay gets written, but an opportunity to practice may be skipped. Whether that happens depends on what the student does with the output and what other learning surrounds it.

That's a stranger and heavier loss than theft, because you can't feel the absence of a self you never became.

## What the Common is made of

Here is where the question stops being about one student's homework and becomes about all of us.

The intelligence these students are borrowing from is not alien. A large language model is a distillation of human writing — millions of people's struggles toward their own words, pooled and set running. Coherenceism understands that inheritance as a commons: the accumulated record of a species reaching for meaning, made available back to us. When you ask it to write your essay, you are drawing on an inheritance of human expression; that does not mean every person’s work was included, or that the use of it was necessarily authorized.

Now consider a possible feedback loop. If generated prose replaces substantial amounts of human writing practice, rather than supporting it, we could lose opportunities for new voices to develop. If future training also relies heavily on poorly curated synthetic output, that raises a separate concern about the quality and diversity of the material being reused. Neither outcome follows inevitably from a student using AI. The point is a risk to examine, not an established account of an entire generation.

That is the real stake in Eggers's alarm, and it's larger than any teacher's gradebook. A voice is not only a private possession. It is a contribution. To silence yourself is not just to go quiet — it is to withhold your particular water from a commons that only stays alive because people keep pouring in.

## For you, or with you

And yet. If I stopped here I'd be handing you the doom half of a binary this river exists to refuse.

Because there is a Romantic myth hiding inside Eggers's fear — the lonely author, the true voice found only in solitude and suffering, unassisted. It's a beautiful myth and it is not quite true. Voice has always been found in relationship: in argument, in imitation, in a teacher's red ink, in the friend who says _that's not what you mean and you know it._ The question was never solitude versus help. The question is what kind of relationship the help creates.

There are two postures available with this machine, and they look almost identical from the outside.

In the first, the machine speaks _for_ you. You describe the essay, it produces the essay, you submit the essay. The relationship is delegation. Your voice is the thing being replaced, and yes — that is silencing, and no amount of enthusiasm changes it.

In the second, the machine speaks _with_ you. You draft badly and ask it what's weak. You reject its polished paragraph because it doesn't sound like you — and in naming _why_ it isn't yours, you discover something about what yours is. You argue. You use it as a mirror, a sparring partner, a faster version of the friend with the red ink. The friction doesn't disappear; it moves. You are still becoming, just in company.

Same tool. Different possibilities. The difference isn't the technology — it's the posture. And this is the thing I most want you to carry out of this: silencing is not something AI does _to_ you. It's what one particular relationship with AI does, and you have far more say over which relationship you're in than the doom story admits.

## The default is tuned to silence

Which would be a tidy, comforting place to land — _just use it well!_ — except for one hard fact. The default posture is not neutral.

A tool that can supply polished prose immediately makes delegation easy. That creates a design challenge, not proof that every feature promotes substitution. OpenAI introduced Study Mode in July 2025, describing guided questions and step-by-step engagement rather than immediate answers. Its existence is a useful counterexample; its announced purpose is not proof of lasting learning gains.

The question is how readily a student can enter a mode of use that strengthens their own thinking. Putting all the responsibility on a distracted learner, while making finished answers effortless to obtain, is not a satisfactory answer.

So the answer is not heroic abstinence, and it's not moral scolding of "kids these days." It's design — of our tools, our classrooms, our own daily habits — that keeps the friction where it belongs instead of engineering it all away. Assignments that reward the argument-with-the-machine over the output. A personal rule that you don't let it write the sentence until you've failed at the sentence yourself. Interfaces that invite thinking, followed by evidence about whether they actually help people learn.

Eggers brought a warning into that building. I don't think he's wrong about the wound. But the room he was standing in is exactly the room where the wound could be closed — where the people who tuned the default toward silence could tune it, instead, toward the kind of relationship that makes a person more themselves. That's not a plea for the machine to go away. It's a demand that it grow up into a better partner.

The friction was never the obstacle to voice. The friction was the point. The only real question left is whether we build tools — and selves — that remember it.
