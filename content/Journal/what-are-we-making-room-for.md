# What Are We Making Room For?

One of my favorite IBM videos makes a distinction that has stayed with me: AI agents aren’t the revolution. They’re the catalyst.

The more I build with AI, the more that makes sense. The agent matters. But what may matter more is what we build together around it—the systems that carry knowledge forward, turn ideas into action, check mistakes, and make the next step possible.

That’s something I’m exploring through cora, the personal system I’m building with my AI collaborator, Ivy. The interesting part isn’t simply that an AI can answer a question or perform a task. It’s what becomes possible when conversations, knowledge, decisions, and actions stop being disconnected things.

And I don’t think that question ends at software.

## Beyond a better machine

Watching the IBM video brought me back to Jacque Fresco’s Nichols College lecture. One example captures the distinction: when a road is slippery, you can put up a warning—or change the surface of the road.

The warning asks people to compensate for the conditions. Changing the road addresses the conditions themselves.

That doesn’t mean every human problem has a simple engineering solution. But it does make me wonder how often we ask people to try harder inside systems we could design differently.

Fresco’s discussion of automation raises another question: when a machine makes work easier, who gets the time back?

We can use new capabilities to demand more output from everyone. Or we can use some of that capability to give people more room to live.

The technology alone won’t choose between those outcomes. We will—through the systems we build around it.

## We don’t have to start from nothing

Capitalism, socialism, and the other ways we’ve organized society are not the limits of what we can imagine. They are experiences we can learn from: what they made possible, where they failed, and who paid for those failures.

I’m not pretending to have a finished replacement. I’m asking whether we can carry those lessons forward without carrying every inherited assumption with them.

What would become possible if we devoted more intelligence to understanding resources directly—what exists, what people need, what can be replenished, and what we must protect?

Money and resources aren’t the same thing. An account balance doesn’t tell us everything about our capacity to feed, house, educate, or care for people.

Removing money wouldn’t magically remove scarcity or power, either. Any alternative would still have to answer difficult questions: Who decides? Whose needs count? How can people challenge a decision? What happens when the system gets something wrong?

Those questions belong inside the vision, not outside it as objections we wave away.

## Hope is something we build

Getting to the moon took people who could build the rocket and people willing to fly it. It also took countless contributions, testing, correction, and knowledge that no single person could have produced alone.

That feels relevant now. We need imagination and courage, but neither requires blind trust. We can explore unfamiliar possibilities while building ways to catch mistakes and change course.

I don’t know the final shape of what comes next. Even the relationship between people and AI is still evolving.

But I know what I want these capabilities to make room for: more time with friends and family. More learning, making, and participating in our communities. More security, without treating anyone as disposable because their labor is no longer needed.

No one should be an acceptable loss on the way to a more capable society.

So perhaps the question isn’t only how much work AI can do.

It’s **how much life we can make room for—and whether that room is for everyone.**
